There is a story the Greeks told about a man who could sing so well that the world stopped to listen. Not as a figure of speech. The story means it literally.

His name was Orpheus. His mother was one of the Muses, which in that world is less a family tree than an explanation. When he sang, rivers slowed in their beds to hear the end of the verse. Trees pulled their roots up out of the ground and walked closer. Stones, which have no ears and no opinions, are said to have wept.

This is the part of the myth everybody remembers. It is not the part the myth is about.

Orpheus married a woman named Eurydice. The stories give her very little. She has no speeches, no arguments, almost no description. She exists, in most tellings, in order to be lost. That is worth noticing before we go on, because it tells you what the story thinks it is doing, and what it is actually doing are two different things.

On their wedding day, Eurydice walked in the long grass by the river. She stepped on a snake. The snake did what snakes do. And by evening the greatest musician in the world was a widower.

Now. Grief does something particular to people, and the Greeks understood this better than we usually give them credit for. Grief makes you certain that there is a thing you could have done. That if you were only clever enough, or fast enough, or good enough at the one thing you happen to be good at, you could undo it.

Orpheus was very good at one thing.

So he went down.

Not metaphorically. He walked to a cave mouth in the south and went down into the country of the dead, carrying nothing but a lyre, which is the single most absurd and most human act in all of Greek myth. He did not bring a sword. He did not bring an army. He brought the thing he was best at, because when you are drowning you reach for what you know.

And here the story does something strange. It works.

He sang to the ferryman, and the ferryman carried him across without payment. He sang to the dog with three heads, and the dog lay down. He sang to the dead themselves, the shades who feel nothing and want nothing, and the dead stopped what they were doing and stood still. The stories say that the wheel of one tormented king paused in its turning. The vulture lifted its head from its work. For the length of one song, the whole machinery of punishment in the underworld simply stopped, because someone had made something beautiful enough to interrupt it.

And then he came to the throne.

Hades and Persephone heard him out. And Persephone, who knows something about being taken below against her will, wept. And they gave him what nobody is ever given. They said yes. Eurydice would follow him back up into the light.

One condition. He must not look back at her until they had both reached the sun.

You already know how this ends. Everyone knows how this ends. And yet the story is told and told again, which means the ending is not the point either. The point is the walk.

Picture it. A long climb in the dark, upward, for hours. And behind him, no sound. The dead do not breathe. They do not scuff the gravel. He cannot hear her, and he has no way to know whether she is there or whether he is climbing alone out of hell for nothing, having been made a fool of by gods who are famous, above all, for making fools of people.

So for hours he walks with a question he cannot answer and cannot stop asking. Is she there. Is she there. Is she there.

And at the very last moment, with the daylight on his own face, he turns around.

She is there. That is the cruelty of it. She was there the whole time. He sees her for a moment, and she is pulled backwards, and this time it is permanent, and it is his own doing.

Now. What is this story for.

The easy reading is that it is a story about obedience. The gods set a rule, the man broke the rule, the man was punished. Plenty of myths run on that engine. But this one does not quite fit, because the rule is a strange one. Why that rule? Not "do not speak to her", not "do not touch her". Do not look.

Here is a better reading. The condition is not a test he failed. The condition is a description of something true.

Orpheus could get almost all the way. He could charm death itself into a concession. What he could not do was walk in the dark and trust that what he had lost was behind him. He needed to see it. He needed proof. And the need for proof is exactly what destroyed the thing he was trying to save.

Anyone who has lost someone knows this walk. You are told the grief will ease. And you keep turning around to check whether it has, and every time you turn around you are back at the start, looking at the thing you lost, watching it recede again. The looking is the mechanism. Not the punishment for it.

Now I want to come back to Eurydice, because I said at the start that the story gives her nothing, and I want to be honest about what that means.

In almost every version, she does not speak. She is bitten, she is mourned, she is fetched, she is lost. She is the reason for the journey and she is never once asked whether she wanted to be on it. A poet two thousand years later noticed this and wrote her differently. In his version she has been dead long enough to have become something else. She is full of her own death the way a fruit is full of its own sweetness. And when the messenger god turns to her and says, he has looked back, she does not understand the sentence. She says, who.

That is a devastating idea and it is worth sitting with. That the person you are climbing out of hell to recover may not be waiting to be recovered. That they may have already gone somewhere you cannot follow, and that your love, which feels enormous and urgent and like the most important fact in the world, may simply not be addressed to anyone any more.

Both readings are in the myth. Orpheus turning around and losing her. Eurydice already gone before he turned. The Greeks were comfortable holding two incompatible things at once, which is something we have largely lost the knack of.

There is one more piece, and most tellings leave it out.

Orpheus went home. He did not stop singing. He sang for years, and the stories say he never took another lover, and that his music after the underworld was better than it had ever been before, which is a very dark thing to say about art and is almost certainly true.

And eventually he was torn apart by a crowd who did not like the way he lived. His head, still singing, was thrown into the river. And the river carried the head out to sea, and it was still singing when it reached the island, and they buried it there, and the nightingales over that grave are said to sing more sweetly than nightingales anywhere else in the world.

So. He did not get her back. He got the song.

That is not a consolation prize, and the Greeks were not offering it as one. They were making a harder claim. That the thing which makes you able to walk into hell is the same thing that makes you unable to leave it behind. That the gift and the wound are not two objects. They are one object, seen from two sides.

Three thousand years later we are still telling it. Every story about a man who goes into the dark to bring someone back is this story. Every story about looking back at the wrong moment is this story.

The Greeks did not think their myths were history. They thought they were instruments. Something you carry with you and use when the situation arises.

And the situation does arise. It arises for everybody, eventually.

So here is the instrument, handed over. There will be a climb, and it will be dark, and you will not be able to hear whether anything is following you.

The story is not telling you that you will manage not to look.

It is telling you that Orpheus did not manage it either. And that he was the best of them.
